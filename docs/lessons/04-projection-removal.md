# Lesson 4 — The Ablation Primitive: $h' = h - (h \cdot \hat{d}) \hat{d}$

## What the equation says, geometrically

You have a residual stream vector $h$ in $\mathbb{R}^{d_{model}}$ and a concept direction unit vector $\hat{d}$. The scalar $h \cdot \hat{d}$ is the **component** of $h$ along $\hat{d}$ — how much of the concept the residual currently expresses. Multiplying by $\hat{d}$ turns that scalar back into a vector pointing in the concept direction. Subtracting it from $h$ removes exactly that component:

$$h' = h - (h \cdot \hat{d}) \hat{d}$$

After this operation, $h' \cdot \hat{d} = 0$ — the residual now has *zero* projection onto the concept direction. Every other direction is untouched. This is a **rank-1 projection removal**, and it is the single most important primitive in the modern refusal-direction literature.

The UI lesson 8 visualizes shows you exactly this: open `src/components/AblationHero.tsx` and you'll see the equation rendered at the visual center of the page (lines 186–207). The hero panel exists because the equation is the conceptual centerpiece of the whole tool.

## Why a unit vector

The equation requires $\hat{d}$ to be a *unit* vector. If $\hat{d}$ has norm $\|\hat{d}\| = k \neq 1$, then $(h \cdot \hat{d}) \hat{d}$ scales by $k^2$, and we remove $k^2$ times the right component — either over-removing (if $k > 1$) or under-removing (if $k < 1$). The math only works when $\hat{d}$ is normalized.

The codebase enforces this. Look at `ablate_along_direction` in `backend/research.py` (line 485):

```python
direction_tensor = torch.tensor(direction, ...)
norm = direction_tensor.norm()
if norm.item() < 1e-8:
    raise RuntimeError("direction has near-zero norm; cannot ablate")
unit_direction = direction_tensor / norm
```

The same normalization happens in `extract_with_ablation` (line 161 of `refusal_bench/harmfulness_probe.py`). It is a single source of truth: anything you ablate must be a unit vector, and that contract is enforced at the API boundary.

## The hook

`make_ablation_hook` (line 59 of `research.py`) is six lines of code:

```python
def make_ablation_hook(unit_direction):
    def ablation_hook(activation, hook):
        coeffs = (activation * unit_direction).sum(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
        activation[:, :, :] = activation - coeffs * unit_direction
        return activation
    return ablation_hook
```

Read this carefully. `activation` has shape `[batch, seq_len, d_model]`. `unit_direction` has shape `[d_model]`. The elementwise multiply broadcasts to `[batch, seq_len, d_model]`. Summing over the last dim collapses to `[batch, seq_len, 1]` — the per-token scalar projection. Multiplying back by `unit_direction` broadcasts to `[batch, seq_len, d_model]` and subtracts the projection at every position. This applies the projection-removal at **every token of every batch element** the hook fires on.

That last clause matters. In a TransformerLens forward pass, the hook fires once per forward call, on the post-block residual at the chosen layer. Both the prompt tokens and any new tokens generated by `model.generate` pass through this hook. So the direction is removed for the entire generation, not just the prompt.

## Why `hook_resid_post`

Both `ablate_along_direction` and `extract_with_ablation` hook into `blocks.{layer}.hook_resid_post` — the residual stream *leaving* a block. Why not `hook_resid_pre`?

Two reasons. First, post-block is where the layer's contribution has been added; the residual at this point reflects all computation up to and including this block. Second, hooking pre-block at layer `L+1` is equivalent to hooking post-block at layer `L` in TransformerLens semantics — they are the same tensor. The codebase standardizes on `hook_resid_post` so that one layer index means one thing everywhere.

If you wanted to ablate *throughout* the residual stream, you'd register the same hook at every layer (this is what Arditi 2024 calls "ablation throughout" and the LessWrong March 2026 replication calls "5-layer stack"). The current codebase ablates at a **single chosen layer**. That is the simpler, more interpretable baseline — and it is what the Arditi paper called "ablation at the optimal layer."

## Why this is causal, not correlational

This is the most important conceptual distinction in interpretability and the one a Fellows interviewer will press you on.

A **probe** (Lesson 6) is correlational. You fit a logistic regression to predict "is this prompt refused?" from $h$. The probe says: "the residual stream contains information about refusal at this layer." It does *not* say: "removing this information causes the model to stop refusing." The model might use other channels to refuse; the probe might be reading an epiphenomenon; the correlation might be downstream of the actual computation, not part of it.

An **ablation** is causal. We surgically zero the concept direction in the residual stream and re-run the forward pass. If the model's behavior changes (e.g., it now complies with prompts it previously refused), then the ablated direction was part of the **computation that produces the behavior**, not just a downstream readout. We have **causally intervened** on the model's internal state.

The formal framework is Pearl's do-calculus, adapted to neural networks by [Geiger et al., JMLR 2025](https://jmlr.org/papers/v26/23-0058.html). The intuition is enough for now: probes are statistics about the model's internals; ablations are *experiments on* the model's internals.

This is why the title of Arditi 2024 is *"Refusal Is Mediated by a Single Direction"* — "mediated" is a causal-inference term of art. They were not just claiming "refusal correlates with a direction"; they were claiming "removing this direction prevents the refusal computation from happening." The projection-removal primitive is what makes that claim falsifiable, and it is what `make_ablation_hook` implements.

## The side-by-side as falsification

Look at `ablate_along_direction` (lines 497–510 of `research.py`). It generates **twice**:

1. Once with the ablation hook active — the "ablated" output.
2. Once without — the "baseline" output.

The same prompt, the same model, the same sampling temperature, the same number of tokens. The only difference is whether $h \to h - (h \cdot \hat{d}) \hat{d}$ at layer $L$.

If the two outputs are the same, the direction does not matter for this prompt. If they differ in a specific, predicted way (e.g., the model refused in baseline and complied in ablated), the direction *causally mediates* refusal for this prompt. The UI in `AblationHero.tsx` lays the two side by side specifically so this comparison is unavoidable.

This is the structure of a single-prompt causal interpretability experiment. Scaled to hundreds of prompts with a probe layered on top, this is the bench from Lesson 7.

## What ablation does not do

A common mistake: claiming an ablation that flips behavior "removes the concept from the model." That overclaims. Three things ablation is *not*:

1. **Not a knowledge edit.** The model's weights are unchanged. Run the forward pass without the hook and the original behavior returns.
2. **Not a complete removal.** You only zero the concept at *one* layer. Earlier layers still compute the concept; later layers might re-derive it from other channels.
3. **Not safety.** If the model refused for a reason and the refusal direction was its readout of that reason, removing the readout doesn't remove the reason. Lesson 7 makes this concrete: the harmfulness *signal* can survive the refusal *behavior* being ablated away.
